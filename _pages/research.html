Research

Making buried polymer interfaces measurable.

Blends, composites and stretchable devices succeed or fail in the few tens of nanometres around a polymer interface, where chain mobility, glass transition, modulus and conductance depart from the bulk. My research is organised around that length scale in three parts. I measure it with instruments that heat one phase and read it where it sits. I make interfaces with light and solvent whose width and chemistry are known in advance, so that the measurements have something to be tested against. And I model how a fast measurement can stand in for a slow one, so that an instrument spends its hours where they count.

01

Measurement science for the polymer interphase

What are the glass transition, modulus and loss tangent of a single phase that is only tens of nanometres wide, and how do you reach that phase when it is buried?

Many of the capabilities soft materials need are latent in instruments that already exist in shared facilities. PRIMA, Photothermal Resonant Infrared Mechanical Analysis, uses the pulsed infrared laser of a commercial AFM-IR instrument as a local heater that acts only on the phase whose vibrational band it is tuned to, with the temperature set by the pulse repetition rate and the mechanical response read by the tip. It resolved the softening of a 50 nm polylactide film and heated one phase of a blend at a time without warming the sample as a whole. PRIMA-nDMA adds a modulated indentation, so that storage and loss moduli are recorded while the laser carries the material through its glass transition, and it followed stress relaxation in a dynamic covalent network, in collaboration with NIST, Bruker and the University of Maryland.

The next steps are a temperature axis calibrated against melting standards, a glass transition read in seconds by sweeping the repetition rate under the modulated probe, access to buried interfaces by tip-based nanomachining that removes a film a few nanometres at a time without altering what it exposes, and the same measurements under strain, light and solvent, which are the conditions in which soft devices fail. Every measurement follows the reporting practice set out in our nanomechanics guide, and the instruments stay within reach of any laboratory with a stock AFM-IR system and a heating stage.

  • PRIMA: Localized Thermomechanical Measurements of Polymers and Blends with AFM-IR, Small 2026
  • PRIMA-nDMA: Spatially-Resolved Viscoelastic Response with AFM-IR, ChemRxiv 2026

02

Interfaces written with light and solvent

Can an interface be written with a stated width and chemistry, and does it then behave the way the chemistry in solution predicts?

Interfaces have their own chemistry. When I mapped wavelength-resolved photochemical reactivity on surfaces during my PhD, the profiles followed the surface absorption spectrum and lost the red shift seen in solution, and two ligation chemistries that behave alike in solution gave very different grafting densities. The same work showed that a single spin-coated blend of two photoreactive polymers forms one network, the other or an interpenetrating network depending only on the wavelength of light, which is the starting point for writing the interface between two networks with a stated width. In the Bao Group I measured the interfaces that decide whether a stretchable device lasts, including a covalently tethered protective layer only about 6 nm thick that kept transistors stable for weeks in water and sweat where thick encapsulants failed, and a solvent-mediated route that writes a functional molecule into a finished polymer film as a gradient or uniformly, set by whether the solvent swells the film.

In the future I want to understand what sets the width and the properties of an interface that is written rather than found. Does the delay between two exposures of a two-colour resin set the interphase between the networks by demixing, and does a joint fail there below a critical width? Does the work function of a contact follow the grafting density of a dipolar layer that light has dosed onto it, up to the point where the chains begin to stretch? And is the photostationary state of a photodynamic network governed by segmental mobility rather than photon flux, which would give a design rule for materials that yield only when light and load coincide? Each of these questions also supplies the measurements above with a specimen whose answer is known in advance.

  • Environmentally Stable and Stretchable Polymer Electronics Enabled by Surface-Tethered Molecular-Level Protection, Nat. Nanotechnol. 2023
  • Mapping Photochemical Reactivity Profiles on Surfaces, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2020

03

More information per instrument hour

Which five samples out of fifty deserve the two-hour measurement, and how do you make the answer reusable by someone who was not in the room?

The measurements that explain a material are slow, and the number of samples that materials discovery needs is large. A surrogate is a fast measurement that stands in for a slow one, an optical photothermal or pump-probe reading of a film in seconds where a nanomechanical map takes hours, a multi-sample array where a single spot was measured before. On its own a surrogate is only a proxy. It becomes useful when the relation to the precise measurement is learned and its limits are known, so that the slow instrument is spent on the few samples where the proxy cannot decide. I treat the two as fidelities of one experiment and score the approach by how many precise measurements it saves on samples it has not seen, which turns the daily choice between high information on few points and high density on many from a habit into a number. The surrogate averages over a thermal diffusion length while the probe reads the top tens of nanometres of one phase, so where the transfer breaks down is as informative as where it holds.

None of this works without data that carry their own context, and most characterization data do not. Our nanomechanics guide sets out how a soft-matter AFM measurement should be reported so that someone else can reuse it; my first review asked the same of surface grafting, where numbers were routinely published without the information needed to compare them; and the self-healing multilayer electronics work showed what a quantified interface width, set between about 13 and 39 nm by processing, is worth at the device level. The aim is a machine-readable record for every measurement, with processing history, calibration, the decision it informed and a status of success or failure, released as open datasets that include the materials that did not work. On that foundation an instrument can begin to propose rather than only record, suggesting the next sample or the next processing condition while the scientist decides, and the same models that guide a measurement campaign can guide the design of new materials in chemistry and materials science, because they are trained on data that say how a material was made, not only what it became.

  • A Guide for Nanomechanical Characterization of Soft Matter via AFM: From Mode Selection to Data Reporting, STAR Protoc. 2025
  • Polymer on Top: Current Limits and Future Perspectives of Quantitatively Evaluating Surface Grafting, Adv. Mater. 2018
  • Autonomous Alignment and Healing in Multilayer Soft Electronics Using Immiscible Dynamic Polymers, Science 2023

Approach

The three parts share one object, the few tens of nanometres around a polymer interface, one materials library, and one data standard. In practice that means choosing the measurement for the question, checking it against an independent method, making specimens whose answer is known before they are measured, and reporting data so that others can reuse it.

Atomic force microscopy

PeakForce QNM, multifrequency and force spectroscopy for quantitative nanomechanics, and tip-based nanomachining for subsurface access.

AFM-IR and nanoscale IR

Contact and resonance-enhanced AFM-IR, PRIMA and PRIMA-nDMA for correlated chemistry, mechanics and transitions.

Surface and polymer chemistry

Grafting, photochemistry and photoresists, with surface analysis, QCM-D and neutron reflectometry.

Data and modeling

Python analysis pipelines, calibration and artifact correction, numerical transport models and multi-fidelity surrogate models.
